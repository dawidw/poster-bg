An image as one idea, drawn with almost nothing.

Geometric abstraction and op art grew in many places, and this project follows one thread of it through Poland. The thread runs through painting, sculpture, textiles and posters: from the constructivists of the 1920s, through Wojciech Fangor's soft rings and Julian Stańczak's vibrating stripes, to the reliefs and woven forms of the 1960s.

Women were part of it from the start. Teresa Żarnower founded Blok, the first Polish constructivist group, in the 1920s, and Katarzyna Kobro built spatial compositions from flat planes. Maria Jarema, a founder of the Kraków Group in the 1930s, drew abstract forms out of music and dance. Later Wanda Gołkowska built reliefs from wooden blocks and Magdalena Abakanowicz hung woven forms from the ceiling.

Posters ran alongside the paintings. After the Second World War Polish graphic designers were given a great deal of artistic freedom, and Jan Lenica later named the result the Polish School of Posters in an article for the Swiss magazine Graphis. It is known for economy of form, wit, hand-made lettering and color used for feeling rather than description. It is usually told as a list of men, but Wanda Telakowska is credited with shaping the contemporary form of a Polish national style in design, and Maria Ihnatowicz, Anna Huskowska and Olga Binder-Siemaszko made posters of their own, though their names appear far less often.

This project borrows techniques, not compositions: soft edges, rhythm, repetition, flat ink. The name comes from Study of Space, the 1958 installation by Wojciech Fangor and Stanisław Zamecznik, the first spatial installation in Poland.
